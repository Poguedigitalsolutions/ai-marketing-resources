# Content Brief Example

This completed example demonstrates how the [Content Brief Template](../templates/content-brief-template.md) can turn customer research and journey insights into a clear, evidence-aware content plan.

The fictional company and customer scenario in this example are not based on a real client.

---

## Example Business

**Business name:** Northstar Operations Group

**Business type:** Veteran-owned operations and process-improvement consultancy

**Primary services:**

- Workflow improvement
- Standard operating procedure development
- Process documentation
- Team training
- Operational readiness assessments
- AI-assisted workflow planning

**Primary audience:** Owners and operations leaders of growing service-based businesses

---

## 1. Content Overview

**Working title:** Fix the Process Before You Buy Another Tool

**Content type:** Educational thought-leadership article

**Primary channel:** LinkedIn article

**Secondary channels:**

- Company website
- Email newsletter
- Writing platform
- Social-media excerpts
- Sales follow-up
- Workshop material

**Content owner:** Founder

**Drafting support:** AI-assisted

**Final approver:** Founder

**Status:** Planned

---

## 2. Business Objective

The article should help Northstar Operations Group:

- Demonstrate practical process-improvement expertise
- Build trust with operations-weary business owners
- Explain why software may not solve an undefined workflow
- Generate qualified conversations
- Support the Operational Bottleneck Assessment offer
- Establish a responsible position on automation and AI

The article should educate first and sell second.

---

## 3. Primary Audience

### Audience Segment

The Operations-Weary Owner

### Typical Role

- Founder
- Owner
- President
- Chief operating officer
- General manager
- Director of operations

### Business Situation

The audience runs a growing service-based business where:

- Employees receive inconsistent instructions
- Important decisions return to the owner
- Handoffs frequently fail
- Critical knowledge is undocumented
- Multiple software tools are already in use
- Automation is being considered
- Operational problems keep returning

---

## 4. Audience Need

The audience needs help determining whether the business has:

- A technology problem
- A process problem
- An ownership problem
- A communication problem
- A training problem
- A combination of several problems

They need a practical way to investigate the workflow before purchasing another tool.

---

## 5. Customer Journey Stage

**Primary stage:** Solution Exploration

**Secondary stage:** Problem Awareness

At this stage, the customer:

- Knows something is slowing the business down
- May believe software is the answer
- Is comparing automation, hiring, consulting, and process documentation
- Wants clarity without unnecessary bureaucracy
- Is skeptical of inflated technology claims

---

## 6. Customer Questions

The article should answer:

- Why did our last software purchase fail?
- How can we tell whether the workflow is the real problem?
- What should we document before automating?
- How do we identify the bottleneck?
- Which decisions should remain human?
- How can we improve the process without creating bureaucracy?
- What should happen when the normal workflow fails?

---

## 7. Customer Friction

The audience may resist the message because:

- Buying software feels faster than mapping a process
- Process documentation sounds tedious
- The owner may believe employees are the problem
- Software vendors may promise rapid transformation
- Internal teams may disagree about how work actually happens
- The business may already have several unused procedures
- The owner may fear that consulting will create more work

The article should acknowledge these concerns rather than dismiss them.

---

## 8. Core Message

Do not automate confusion.

Before purchasing another tool, define:

- What starts the process
- Who owns each step
- What information is required
- Where decisions occur
- Who approves the result
- What happens when the normal path fails

Technology should support a clear workflow, not substitute for one.

---

## 9. Supporting Messages

### Message 1: Repeated mistakes may be system signals

When several people make the same mistake, the process deserves inspection.

### Message 2: Software cannot resolve unclear ownership

A platform may move information, but it cannot decide who should be accountable unless the business defines that responsibility.

### Message 3: Automation increases the speed of the existing process

A flawed process may fail faster after automation.

### Message 4: Human approval remains necessary

Important customer communications, exceptions, financial decisions, and high-risk actions should retain human oversight.

### Message 5: Start with one workflow

The audience does not need to redesign the entire business at once.

---

## 10. Desired Reader Outcome

After reading, the audience should be able to:

1. Recognize the difference between a tool problem and a workflow problem
2. Identify one process worth examining
3. Ask five practical workflow questions
4. Delay unnecessary software purchases
5. Understand where human approval belongs
6. Consider an operational bottleneck assessment

---

## 11. Primary Call to Action

> Choose one workflow that repeatedly slows down, breaks, or returns to you. Map what happens before buying another tool.

---

## 12. Secondary Call to Action

> Tell us where work waits, repeats, or disappears in your business.

This may lead naturally to a conversation about an Operational Bottleneck Assessment.

The call to action should remain low-pressure and useful even when the reader does not become a lead.

---

## 13. Offer Connection

### Relevant Offer

Operational Bottleneck Assessment

### Possible Deliverables

- Discovery conversation
- Current workflow review
- Bottleneck identification
- Ownership review
- Risk summary
- Priority recommendations
- Suggested next steps

### Offer Transition

The article should not abruptly turn into a sales pitch.

Suggested transition:

> Sometimes the hardest part is seeing the process clearly while you are still inside it. An outside assessment can help identify where information, decisions, and ownership become tangled.

## 16. Claims to Verify

Before publication, verify any claims involving:

- Cost savings
- Productivity gains
- Error reduction
- Employee performance
- Automation outcomes
- Industry statistics
- Software adoption rates
- Customer experience improvements
- Compliance or legal requirements

Avoid using precise numerical claims unless the source directly supports them.

---

## 17. Claims to Avoid

Do not claim that:

- Process mapping guarantees success
- Every software purchase is unnecessary
- Automation always creates risk
- Employees are never responsible for mistakes
- One method works for every business
- Northstar can eliminate human error
- The assessment guarantees cost savings
- AI can independently design a safe workflow

---

## 18. Brand Voice

The article should sound:

- Clear
- Practical
- Calm
- Confident
- Respectful
- Human
- Prepared
- Evidence-aware

It should not sound:

- Alarmist
- Anti-technology
- Condescending
- Militaristic
- Overly technical
- Sales-heavy
- Trend-chasing
- Generic

---

Use language such as:

- Bottleneck
- Ownership
- Handoff
- Decision point
- Approval
- Failure path
- Human review
- Real conditions

- Do not automate confusion

Avoid:

- Revolutionary
- Game-changing
- Effortless
- Fully autonomous

## 21. Recommended Article Structure

### Opening: The Expensive Shortcut

Open with a recognizable situation:

A business owner purchases another software platform because work keeps slowing down.

The new system is installed.

The delays remain.

Now the business has the same confusion spread across another tool.

### Section 1: The Tool May Not Be the Problem

Explain that technology can support a workflow, but it cannot define unclear responsibilities, approvals, or exceptions.

### Section 2: Find the Real Bottleneck

Introduce the idea that bottlenecks may appear where:

- Work waits
- Information disappears
- Decisions return to the owner
- Employees repeat tasks
- Approvals become unclear
- Exceptions have no path

### Section 3: Ask Five Questions

Provide five practical questions:

1. What starts the process?
2. Who owns each step?
3. What information is required?
4. Who approves the result?
5. What happens when the normal path fails?

### Section 4: Decide What Technology Should Do

Explain that technology may help:

- Move information
- Trigger reminders
- Route tasks
- Maintain records
- Track status
- Report exceptions

Technology should not independently make high-risk decisions without appropriate review.

### Section 5: Start Small

Encourage the reader to choose one high-friction workflow rather than redesign the whole company.

### Conclusion: Build Clarity Before Speed

Close with the principle:

> A faster process is not automatically a better process. Build clarity first. Then decide where technology belongs.

### Final Call to Action

> Choose one workflow that keeps returning to you. Map it before buying another tool.

## 22. Opening Draft Direction

Possible opening:

> The software demonstration looked perfect.
>
> Tasks moved automatically. Notifications appeared at the right time. Dashboards showed every stage of the process.
>
> Three months later, the team was still asking the same questions.
>
> The problem was never the absence of software.
>
> The problem was that no one had agreed on what the process was supposed to do.

This opening should be treated as fictional unless supported by a real, authorized story.

---

## 23. Story Guidance

A story may be included when it:

- Illustrates a real workflow problem
- Protects privacy
- Preserves relevant context
- Separates fact from reconstruction
- Avoids blaming employees
- Connects clearly to the lesson

Do not:

- Invent a client
- Present reconstructed dialogue as exact
- Exaggerate consequences
- imply verified results without evidence
- Identify a company without permission

---

## 24. Visual Suggestions

Possible supporting visuals include:

- Simple workflow diagram
- Bottleneck illustration
- Five-question process map
- Human approval checkpoint diagram
- Before-and-after workflow comparison

Visuals should:

- Use readable text
- Include alternative text
- Avoid dense diagrams
- Match the article’s practical tone
- Avoid implying guaranteed transformation

---

## 25. Accessibility Requirements

Confirm:

- Headings follow a logical order
- Links use descriptive text
- Paragraphs remain readable
- Lists are used appropriately
- Images include alternative text
- Diagrams are explained in surrounding text
- Color is not the only way information is communicated
- Mobile formatting remains usable

---

## 26. Privacy Requirements

Do not include:

- Client names without permission
- Employee names
- Internal screenshots
- Proprietary workflows
- Customer records
- Credentials
- Financial details
- Confidential performance data
- Private messages
- Identifying operational incidents

Use fictional or anonymized examples where necessary.

---

## 27. Responsible AI Requirements

AI may assist with:

- Organizing the brief
- Developing an outline
- Comparing source documents
- Drafting alternatives
- Identifying repetition
- Creating repurposing ideas
- Checking structural completeness

AI must not:

- Invent evidence
- Create fake customer quotations
- Add unsupported statistics
- Publish automatically
- Present assumptions as facts
- expose confidential information
- Replace final human judgment

---

## 28. Human Review Checklist

Before approval, a person must verify:

- [ ] The article answers the intended customer questions
- [ ] The central message remains clear
- [ ] Important claims are supported
- [ ] Assumptions are labeled
- [ ] Examples are fictional, anonymized, or authorized
- [ ] Quotations are genuine and approved
- [ ] The article reflects the brand voice
- [ ] The article does not blame employees without evidence
- [ ] Privacy is protected
- [ ] Accessibility has been reviewed
- [ ] The call to action is appropriate
- [ ] The live links work
- [ ] The final version has explicit approval

## 33. Risks and Limitations

### Risk: Sounding Anti-Technology

Safeguard:

Make clear that technology is useful when it supports a defined process.

### Risk: Oversimplifying Operational Problems

Safeguard:

Acknowledge that problems may involve leadership, training, workload, systems, ownership, and behavior.

### Risk: Blaming Software Vendors

Safeguard:

Focus on internal readiness and requirements rather than assigning blame.

### Risk: Presenting Fictional Examples as Real

Safeguard:

Label fictional scenarios clearly.

### Risk: Overpromising the Assessment

Safeguard:

Describe deliverables without guaranteeing outcomes.
