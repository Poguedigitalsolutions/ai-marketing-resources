# Customer Avatar Research Example

This completed example demonstrates how the [Customer Avatar Research Prompt](../prompts/customer-avatar-research.md) can be used to organize customer evidence without relying on stereotypes, invented certainty, or unsupported quotations.

The fictional company and research participants in this example are not real clients or customers.

---

## Example Business

**Business name:** Northstar Operations Group

**Business type:** Veteran-owned operations and process-improvement consultancy

**Primary services:**

- Workflow improvement
- Standard operating procedure development
- Process documentation
- Team training
- Operational readiness assessments
- AI-assisted workflow planning

**Primary market:** Small and midsize service-based businesses

---

## 1. Research Objective

The purpose of this research is to understand:

- Why growing service-business owners seek operational help
- Where they experience workflow friction
- How they currently attempt to solve process problems
- What prevents them from hiring a consultant
- What evidence builds trust
- What outcomes matter most
- What language they use to describe their problems
- How they move from awareness to a buying decision

---

## 2. Research Questions

The research should answer:

1. What operational problems create the most frustration?
2. What events trigger the search for outside help?
3. What solutions have customers already tried?
4. Why did those solutions fail or remain incomplete?
5. What risks do customers associate with consulting?
6. What outcomes would make the engagement worthwhile?
7. Who influences or approves the decision?
8. What language do customers use when describing the problem?
9. What proof do they need before moving forward?
10. What could prevent them from completing the purchase?

---

## 3. Research Method

This fictional example assumes the following research process:

- Eight customer interviews
- Three lost-opportunity interviews
- Review of fifteen sales-call notes
- Review of twenty customer-support conversations
- Review of ten public online discussions about operational problems
- Review of website analytics and lead-form questions

Because this is a demonstration, the findings below are simulated rather than collected from real participants.

No statement in this example should be presented as real customer research.

---

## 4. Evidence Classification

Each finding should be classified as one of the following:

### Direct Evidence

Information collected directly from an eligible customer, prospect, or documented interaction.

### Behavioral Evidence

Actions such as repeated delays, abandoned forms, missed approvals, repeated support requests, or purchasing behavior.

### Pattern

A finding observed across several independent sources.

### Minority Viewpoint

A meaningful perspective expressed by a smaller number of participants.

### Assumption

A belief that has not yet been adequately supported.

### Working Hypothesis

A provisional interpretation that should be tested with additional research.

## 5. Primary Customer Segment

### Segment Name

The Operations-Weary Owner

### Description

The Operations-Weary Owner runs a growing service-based business with approximately 10 to 75 employees.

The business has grown beyond informal coordination, but many critical processes still depend on memory, direct supervision, spreadsheets, chat messages, or one experienced employee.

The owner is often involved in daily problem-solving and believes the business should be able to operate more consistently without requiring constant intervention.

---

## 6. Business Context

Typical characteristics include:

- Annual revenue between approximately $1 million and $15 million
- Ten to seventy-five employees
- Several departments or functional teams
- Repeated customer-service or delivery issues
- One or more key employees holding undocumented knowledge
- Heavy reliance on the owner for decisions
- Multiple software tools with weak integration
- Inconsistent onboarding
- Limited process documentation
- Interest in automation without a clear implementation plan

These characteristics are research filters, not requirements for every customer.

## 12. Common Problems

### Problem 1: The Owner Is the Default Decision-Maker

Many routine questions return to the owner because approval rules are unclear or undocumented.

Consequences may include:

- Delays
- Interruptions
- Employee hesitation
- Owner exhaustion
- Slow customer response
- Dependence on one person

### Problem 2: Critical Knowledge Lives in People’s Heads

Experienced employees know how work should be completed, but the process is not fully documented.

Consequences may include:

- Inconsistent training
- Risk when someone leaves
- Different versions of the same process
- Repeated questions
- Slow onboarding
- Operational vulnerability

### Problem 3: Handoffs Fail

Information moves between departments without clear ownership or completion criteria.

Consequences may include:

- Missed deadlines
- Duplicate work
- Customer frustration
- Conflicting records
- Tasks falling between teams
- Blame between departments

### Problem 4: Automation Was Added Too Early

The business purchased software or built automation before defining the actual workflow.

Consequences may include:

- Faster execution of a flawed process
- Confusing alerts
- Duplicate records
- Incorrect routing
- Low employee adoption
- Additional manual work

### Problem 5: Employees Receive Inconsistent Instructions

Managers teach different versions of the process.

Consequences may include:

- Uneven performance
- Employee frustration
- Difficult accountability
- Customer inconsistency
- Conflict between teams

Customers may begin looking for help after:

- A key employee resigns
- A major customer complains
- An important deadline is missed
- The company opens another location
- Revenue grows faster than operational capacity
- A new manager discovers undocumented processes
- The owner becomes unable to take time away
- An audit identifies weak controls
- An automation project fails
- Customer experience becomes inconsistent
- Employee turnover increases
- The company prepares for acquisition, succession, or expansion

Customers may have tried:

- Writing procedures themselves
- Purchasing project-management software
- Adding more meetings
- Hiring an operations manager
- Asking employees to document their work
- Creating spreadsheets
- Recording training videos
- Using AI to draft procedures
- Buying workflow automation tools
- Copying a process from another company

## 15. Why Previous Attempts Failed

Common reasons include:

- No clear owner
- Lack of employee participation
- Documentation became outdated
- The process was copied instead of observed
- Software was selected before requirements were defined
- Procedures were too long or difficult to use
- Managers continued teaching different methods
- No approval process existed
- No one tested the workflow under real conditions
- The owner remained the final answer for every exception
- The solution focused on tools rather than behavior and responsibility

## 16. Customer Frustrations

- Answering the same questions repeatedly
- Discovering problems after the customer complains
- Employees blaming one another
- Paying for software no one uses
- Being unable to leave the business
- Recreating information
- Finding different versions of documents
- Waiting for approvals
- Training people repeatedly
- Watching growth create more disorder
- Feeling that every improvement project becomes another project to manage

## 17. Simulated Customer Language

The following statements are fictional examples created for demonstration.

They are not real quotations and must not be presented as customer evidence.

> I feel like every decision eventually ends up back on my desk.

> We have procedures, but I do not know whether anyone is using the current version.

> The business runs well when our most experienced employee is here.

> We bought software to fix the process, but now we have the same problem in three systems.

> I do not want more bureaucracy. I just want people to know what happens next.

> Every department believes the delay belongs to someone else.

> I cannot tell whether this is a people problem or a process problem.

> I want to automate, but I do not want customers trapped inside a bad system.

These statements illustrate the type of language researchers should capture and verify during real interviews.

## 20. Buying Objections

### Objection: Consulting Will Create More Work

The customer may believe the engagement will require excessive meetings, documentation, and employee time.

Response approach:

Explain the scope, participation requirements, and how the work will reduce future confusion.

### Objection: The Consultant Will Not Understand the Business

The customer may fear receiving generic recommendations.

Response approach:

Describe the observation, interview, documentation, and testing process.

### Objection: Employees Will Resist

The customer may believe employees will reject new processes.

Response approach:

Include employees in process discovery and test recommendations with the people performing the work.

### Objection: The Business Is Too Small

The customer may believe process improvement is only for large organizations.

Response approach:

Focus on high-impact workflows rather than building unnecessary bureaucracy.

### Objection: The Business Needs Software, Not Consulting

The customer may believe purchasing another tool is the fastest solution.

Response approach:

Explain that software should support a defined workflow rather than substitute for one.

### Objection: The Cost Is Too High

The customer may compare the engagement cost only to the price of software or internal labor.

Response approach:

Connect the work to delays, rework, missed opportunities, employee dependence, customer loss, and operational risk.

## 21. Trust Builders

The customer may be more likely to trust a provider who demonstrates:

- Practical experience
- Clear methods
- Realistic claims
- Respect for employees
- Transparent pricing
- Defined deliverables
- Strong confidentiality practices
- Human approval requirements
- Familiarity with operational risk
- Willingness to identify what should not be automated
- Completed examples
- References or authorized case studies
- Clear ownership and follow-up

---

## 22. Trust Breakers

Trust may be reduced by:

- Guaranteed results
- Aggressive sales pressure
- Excessive military language
- Blaming employees
- Generic AI-generated recommendations
- Claims that everything should be automated
- Weak privacy practices
- Unclear deliverables
- Hidden costs
- Recommendations made before observing the workflow
- Overly technical explanations
- Failure to acknowledge uncertainty

---

6. Quality of deliverables

## 24. Buying Committee

A typical decision may involve:

### Economic Buyer

Owner, president, or chief operating officer

### Operational Champion

Operations manager, department head, or project lead

### Technical Reviewer

Information technology or systems administrator

### Risk Reviewer

Legal, compliance, privacy, or human resources

### Affected Users

Employees who perform the workflow

The buyer may approve the engagement, but employee adoption strongly influences the result.

## 25. Customer Journey

### Stage 1: Problem Awareness

The customer notices:

- Repeated mistakes
- Slow handoffs
- Owner overload
- Inconsistent results
- Employee confusion

Customer question:

> Why does this problem keep happening?

Helpful content:

- Bottleneck identification guides
- Process-versus-people explanations
- Workflow warning signs
- Operational readiness assessments

### Stage 2: Solution Awareness

The customer begins exploring:

- Process documentation
- Consulting
- Automation
- New software
- Training
- Hiring an operations leader

Customer question:

> What kind of solution do we actually need?

Helpful content:

- Workflow mapping examples
- Software-before-process warnings
- Process-improvement frameworks
- Comparison guides

### Stage 3: Provider Evaluation

The customer compares providers.

Customer questions:

- Will they understand our business?
- How much time will this require?
- Will employees cooperate?
- What will we receive?
- How will confidential information be protected?
- What happens after implementation?

Helpful content:

- Methodology explanation
- Completed examples
- Scope and deliverable descriptions
- Privacy guidance
- Case studies
- Implementation timelines

### Stage 4: Decision

The customer needs confidence that:

- The project is practical
- The scope is clear
- The provider is credible
- The work will not create unnecessary bureaucracy
- The team will be involved
- The system will remain maintainable

Helpful content:

- Proposal
- Statement of work
- Responsibilities
- Timeline
- Approval process
- Success measures

### Stage 5: Onboarding

The customer needs:

- Clear expectations
- Required documents
- Interview schedule
- Confidentiality boundaries
- Access instructions
- Project ownership
- Communication cadence

### Stage 6: Implementation

The customer needs:

- Progress visibility
- Draft review
- Employee involvement
- Testing
- Approval checkpoints
- Training
- Change documentation

### Stage 7: Adoption and Retention

The customer needs:

- Follow-up
- Performance review
- Updates
- Version control
- Refresher training
- Correction procedures
- Ongoing improvement

### Stage 8: Advocacy

The customer may provide:

- Testimonials
- Referrals
- Case-study participation
- Introductions
- Feedback
- Additional project opportunities

Permission must be obtained before using customer names, quotations, results, or identifying details.

## 27. Message Themes

### Theme 1: Fix the Process Before Adding the Tool

Technology should support a defined workflow.

### Theme 2: Clarity Reduces Friction

Clear ownership, instructions, and approval rules improve performance.

### Theme 3: Systems Should Support People

Process improvement should make work easier to understand and perform.

### Theme 4: Prepare Before Pressure Arrives

Readiness reduces the impact of turnover, growth, emergencies, and unexpected demand.

### Theme 5: Human Approval Still Matters

Important decisions and customer communications require accountability.

## 29. Research Gaps

The following questions require additional evidence:

- Which trigger event produces the strongest buying urgency?
- Which industries experience the highest cost from undocumented processes?
- Which objections most often prevent a purchase?
- What project price range feels reasonable?
- How much employee participation is acceptable?
- Which deliverables create the greatest perceived value?
- Does veteran-owned status meaningfully influence trust?
- Which content topics generate qualified inquiries?
- What terminology feels clearest to customers?
- How strongly do customers value responsible AI safeguards?

These should remain research questions rather than being treated as facts.

---

## 30. Assumptions Requiring Validation

Current assumptions include:

- Owners recognize operational problems before employees do
- Most buyers want automation
- Veteran-owned positioning increases trust
- Process documentation is viewed positively
- Owners understand the difference between workflows and procedures
- Customers are willing to involve frontline employees
- Buyers will pay for discovery before implementation
- Responsible AI controls influence purchasing decisions

Each assumption should be tested through interviews, behavior, or sales evidence.

---

## 31. Minority and Contradictory Viewpoints

Some customers may:

- Prefer software over consulting
- Want the owner to remain involved in every decision
- View documentation as unnecessary
- Resist employee participation
- Prioritize speed over governance
- Want full automation
- Prefer informal communication
- Believe the problem is entirely employee-related
- Reject outside review

These perspectives should not be discarded merely because they conflict with the preferred service model.

They may reveal:

- Poor audience fit
- Different service needs
- Messaging gaps
- Risk factors
- Product opportunities
- Reasons not to pursue the engagement

## 33. Human Review Requirements

A person must review:

- Participant eligibility
- Consent
- Privacy
- Exact quotations
- Research notes
- Evidence classification
- Findings
- Contradictions
- Recommendations
- Public claims
- Customer identification
- Marketing applications
- AI-generated summaries

AI may help organize information, but it should not decide which findings are true without human verification.

---

## 34. Privacy and Consent

Real customer research should document:

- Participant consent
- Recording permission
- Quotation permission
- Attribution preferences
- Confidentiality requirements
- Data storage
- Access controls
- Retention period
- Deletion process
- Approved uses

Do not place private customer information into public repositories.
