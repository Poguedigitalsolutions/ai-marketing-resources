# Approval Workflow Example

This completed example demonstrates how the [Approval Workflow Template](../templates/approval-workflow-template.md) and [Human Approval Before Execution Framework](../frameworks/human-approval-before-execution.md) can be used to manage review, risk, revisions, authorization, emergency pauses, and accountability.

The fictional company, campaign, roles, and approval records in this example are not based on a real client.

---

**Business type:** Veteran-owned operations and process-improvement consultancy

**Primary campaign:** Fix the Process Before the Tool

**Primary offer:** Operational Bottleneck Assessment

**Workflow owner:** Founder

**Status:** Example only

## 1. Purpose

This workflow ensures that public content, automated communications, customer-facing materials, operational recommendations, and AI-assisted outputs receive the appropriate level of human review before publication or execution.

The workflow is designed to:

- Prevent unsupported claims
- Protect confidential information
- Preserve brand voice
- Confirm consent and permissions
- Identify accessibility concerns
- Match review effort to risk
- Prevent unauthorized automation
- Record revisions and approvals
- Support emergency pauses
- Preserve human accountability

AI may prepare material.

A person must authorize important actions.

---

## 2. Scope

This approval workflow applies to:

- Articles
- Social media posts
- Email campaigns
- Lead magnets
- Landing pages
- Webinar materials
- Proposals
- Case studies
- Customer quotations
- Automated communications
- Workflow recommendations
- AI-generated images
- Public reports
- Campaign changes
- High-impact personalization
- Customer-facing automation

It may also be adapted for internal documentation.

---

## 3. Approval Principles

### Human Accountability

A named person remains responsible for the final decision.

### Exact-Version Review

Approval applies only to the exact version reviewed.

Material changes require reapproval.

### Risk-Based Review

Higher-risk content requires more review, stronger evidence, and additional approval.

### Separation of Preparation and Authorization

The person or system preparing an asset should not automatically authorize high-risk execution.

### Documented Decisions

Important approvals, rejections, conditions, and revisions should be recorded.

### Pause Before Harm

Any reviewer may pause execution when a serious concern appears.

---

## 4. Risk Levels

### Level 1: Low Risk

Examples:

- General educational social posts
- Routine blog updates
- Internal planning notes
- Non-sensitive formatting changes
- Previously approved content reused without material changes

Required review:

- Brand voice
- Accuracy
- Links
- Formatting
- Final owner approval

### Level 2: Moderate Risk

Examples:

- Email campaigns
- Lead magnets
- Landing pages
- Webinar materials
- Promotional content
- Customer-facing automation
- Public research summaries
- New claims or offers

Required review:

- Accuracy
- Sources
- Brand voice
- Privacy
- Consent
- Accessibility
- Automation logic
- Final approval

### Level 3: High Risk

Examples:

- Legal, financial, medical, employment, or compliance claims
- Sensitive customer communications
- Personalized recommendations
- Customer case studies
- Public incident communications
- Automated decisions
- Materials using confidential information
- AI-generated depictions of real people
- Major pricing or contractual statements

Required review:

- Subject-matter expert
- Privacy or legal review when appropriate
- Senior decision-maker approval
- Documented evidence
- Exact-version signoff
- Monitoring and rollback plan

### Level 4: Prohibited or Unacceptable

Examples:

- Fabricated customer quotations
- Deceptive impersonation
- Unauthorized disclosure of private information
- Unsupported guarantees
- Discriminatory targeting
- Automated high-impact decisions without review
- Content using stolen or unlicensed material
- Communications sent without required consent
- AI outputs presented as verified evidence without validation

Required action:

- Do not publish
- Do not execute
- Record the rejection
- Escalate when necessary
- Correct or retire the material

## 5. Roles

### Requestor

The person asking for the asset or action.

Responsibilities:

- Define the objective
- Identify the audience
- Provide source material
- State deadlines
- Identify risks
- Confirm intended use

### Creator

The person or system preparing the draft.

Responsibilities:

- Follow the approved brief
- Use authorized sources
- Label assumptions
- Avoid confidential information
- Record AI assistance
- Submit the exact version for review

### Primary Reviewer

Responsibilities:

- Review accuracy
- Check brand voice
- Verify usefulness
- Identify missing context
- Confirm links and formatting
- Request revisions

### Specialist Reviewer

May include:

- Legal counsel
- Privacy reviewer
- Accessibility reviewer
- Human resources
- Compliance reviewer
- Information security
- Subject-matter expert

Responsibilities:

- Review issues within their expertise
- Approve, reject, or conditionally approve
- Document concerns and required changes

### Final Approver

Responsibilities:

- Confirm required reviews are complete
- Authorize the exact version
- Confirm timing and channel
- Confirm rollback or correction plan
- Accept accountability for execution

### Publisher or Operator

Responsibilities:

- Publish only the approved version
- Confirm live presentation
- Monitor for errors
- Pause when instructed
- Preserve publication records

---

## 6. Approval Statuses

Each asset must have one of these statuses:

- Draft
- In review
- Revision required
- Conditionally approved
- Approved
- Rejected
- Paused
- Published
- Corrected
- Retired

No asset may move directly from Draft to Published without required review.

---

## 7. Standard Workflow

### Step 1: Submit the Request

The requestor provides:

- Asset name
- Objective
- Audience
- Channel
- Deadline
- Offer or message
- Source material
- Known risks
- Required reviewers
- Intended automation

### Step 2: Assign Risk Level

The workflow owner classifies the asset as:

- Low risk
- Moderate risk
- High risk
- Prohibited

When uncertain, use the higher reasonable risk level.

### Step 3: Prepare the Draft

The creator:

- Uses approved sources
- Records assumptions
- Protects private information
- Follows brand voice guidance
- Notes AI assistance
- Identifies unresolved questions

### Step 4: Conduct Primary Review

The primary reviewer checks:

- Purpose
- Audience
- Accuracy
- Sources
- Claims
- Brand voice
- Privacy
- Permissions
- Accessibility
- Call to action
- Channel requirements
- Automation behavior

### Step 5: Conduct Specialist Review

Specialist review is required when the content involves:

- Legal or regulatory issues
- Sensitive personal data
- Employment decisions
- Financial claims
- Medical or safety information
- Customer identification
- High-impact automation
- Accessibility risk
- Contractual commitments

### Step 6: Revise

The creator addresses reviewer comments.

Each revision should include:

- Version number
- Date
- Change summary
- Person making the change
- Reviewer requiring the change

### Step 7: Final Approval

The final approver confirms:

- Required reviews are complete
- All conditions were resolved
- The exact version is identified
- Publication details are correct
- Monitoring is assigned
- A pause or correction path exists

### Step 8: Publish or Execute

The operator:

- Uses the approved version
- Confirms links, files, timing, audience, and settings
- Records publication
- Verifies the live result

### Step 9: Monitor

Monitor:

- Errors
- Complaints
- Broken links
- Privacy concerns
- Accessibility issues
- Unexpected automation
- Customer confusion
- Unsupported interpretations

### Step 10: Correct or Retire

When a problem is found:

- Pause when necessary
- Preserve evidence
- Assess severity
- Correct the issue
- Obtain reapproval
- Notify affected people when appropriate
- Document the resolution

## 11. Conditional Approval

Conditional approval may be used when:

- The remaining change is specific
- The condition is documented
- The condition can be verified
- No high-risk uncertainty remains
- Final execution is blocked until the condition is complete

Example:

> Approved provided the privacy notice is added and verified before launch.

Conditional approval must not become a shortcut around unresolved risk.

---

## 12. Rejection

An asset should be rejected when:

- Claims are unsupported
- Consent is missing
- Confidential information is exposed
- The audience is inappropriate
- The content is deceptive
- Required permissions are absent
- Reviewers cannot verify critical facts
- The automation creates unacceptable risk
- The content conflicts with brand values
- The asset uses prohibited material

A rejection record should include:

- Reason
- Reviewer
- Date
- Evidence
- Whether revision is permitted
- Required next step

---

## 13. Reapproval Triggers

A previously approved asset must return to review when:

- Claims change
- Pricing changes
- The audience changes
- The offer changes
- New personalization is added
- Automation logic changes
- New data sources are used
- Legal or platform requirements change
- A privacy concern is discovered
- A complaint reveals confusion
- A new AI model materially changes the output
- The asset is repurposed into a higher-risk channel
- The approved version is edited after approval

Minor spelling corrections may follow a documented expedited review process.

---

## 14. Emergency Pause

Any reviewer or authorized operator may pause execution when:

- Sensitive information is exposed
- Messages are sent to the wrong audience
- Consent fails
- An incorrect claim is discovered
- A broken link creates harm
- Automation behaves unexpectedly
- A complaint indicates serious risk
- A system security problem appears
- The approved version was not used
- Legal or compliance concerns arise

### Emergency Pause Actions

1. Stop publication or automation
2. Preserve evidence
3. Record the time and scope
4. Notify the workflow owner
5. Identify affected people
6. Assess severity
7. Correct the issue
8. Obtain reapproval
9. Verify the fix
10. Document lessons

---

## 15. Rollback Plan

For the fictional email sequence:

- Pause all scheduled emails
- Remove affected contacts from active enrollment
- Correct the approved content
- Test the corrected version
- Obtain reapproval
- Restart only when authorized
- Notify recipients when a correction is necessary
- Preserve the original and corrected versions

## 17. Approval Metrics

Possible measures include:

- Review completion time
- Number of revision cycles
- Errors caught before publication
- Errors found after publication
- Approval delays
- Conditional approvals
- Emergency pauses
- Reapproval frequency
- Broken-link rate
- Privacy incidents
- Accessibility findings
- Percentage of assets using the approved version

Fast approval is not automatically good approval.

The goal is timely, proportionate, accountable review.

---

## 18. Anti-Rubber-Stamping Controls

To prevent superficial approval:

- Reviewers must have access to the full context
- Approval must identify the exact version
- Checklists must include comments when concerns exist
- High-risk assets require specialist review
- Reviewers may reject unrealistic deadlines
- Repeated approval without comments may be audited
- AI-generated review summaries cannot replace human review
- The final approver must confirm unresolved conditions are closed

---

## 19. AI Use in the Approval Process

AI may assist with:

- Comparing versions
- Identifying missing sections
- Flagging possible unsupported claims
- Checking link lists
- Organizing reviewer comments
- Summarizing revision history
- Preparing review checklists
- Identifying inconsistent language

AI must not:

- Provide final approval
- Decide that consent is valid
- Make legal determinations
- Approve customer quotations
- Authorize publication
- Hide unresolved concerns
- Replace specialist judgment
- Alter an approved version without review
